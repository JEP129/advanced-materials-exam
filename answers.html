1. Thermal transitions

Q21What's the definition of state function? Give the physical meaning of the second-order transition in terms of ΔV and ΔH?

State function: a property that depends only on the present state, not on the path (U, H, S, G, V; not Q or W).

  1. ① Crystal: V rises slowly with T.
  2. ② At Tm, V and H jump → first-order transition (ΔV ≠ 0, ΔH ≠ 0, latent heat).
  3. ③ Liquid: larger slope (free volume grows).
  4. ④ Fast cooling: the liquid line continues below Tm (supercooled liquid).
  5. ⑤ At Tg the line only bends: ΔV = 0, ΔH = 0 (chains are frozen in place, no rearrangement), but the slopes α = (1/V)(∂V/∂T) and Cp = (∂H/∂T) change → second-order transition.
  6. ⑥ (dashed) the liquid line if it were not frozen.

Q9How to determine the Tg of an amorphous polymer (give at least 2 ways)? Why a peak appears at the Tg point as the DMA spectrum was shown?

Ways to determine Tg:

  1. ① Dilatometry (V–T): the slope of V vs T changes at Tg.
  2. ② DSC: a step (ΔCp) in the heat-capacity baseline at Tg.
  3. ③ DMA: G′ drops ~10³-fold from glassy to rubbery; tan δ peaks at Tg.

Why a DMA peak: in the glass-transition region cooperative segmental motion has just started but is sluggish, so it lags the oscillation and dissipates the most energy; G″ peaks while G′ falls, so tan δ = G″/G′ shows a maximum (τ ≈ 1/ω). See the sketch in Q37.

Q37Why a peak appears at the Tg point as the DMA spectrum was shown?

  1. ① Below Tg (glassy): segments are frozen → G′ high, little energy loss → tan δ small.
  2. ② At Tg: cooperative segmental motion starts but is sluggish (τ ≈ 1/ω) → segments move yet lag the load → maximum energy dissipated as heat (G″ peaks) while G′ drops ~10³-fold → tan δ = G″/G′ peaks.
  3. ③ Above Tg (rubbery): segments follow the load easily → loss small again → tan δ falls.

Q19The melting temperature of PVDF is 174 °C, please estimate its Tg value.

Tg ≈ 224 K (≈ −49 °C).

Empirical rule: Tg ≈ ½–⅔ Tm (in K). Tm = 174 + 273 = 447 K. PVDF is symmetric → use ½: Tg ≈ 447/2 ≈ 224 K (tabulated ≈ −45 °C).

Q8Please use Simha-Boyer equation to estimate the thermal expansion coefficient αg of PS below Tg (Tg = 373 K, αl = 5.5×10−4 K−1)

αg ≈ 2.47×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.

Simha–Boyer: (αl − αg)Tg = 0.113
αg = αl − 0.113/Tg = 5.5×10⁻⁴ − 0.113/373 = 5.5×10⁻⁴ − 3.03×10⁻⁴ = 2.47×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹

2. Structure and Tg

Q4Which one of the following polymers has the highest Tg (1) PS (2) Poly(vinyl biphenyl) (3) Poly(vinyl naphthalene), explain.

(2) Poly(vinyl biphenyl), Tg ≈ 145 °C.

The bulkier the pendant group, the greater the steric hindrance to bond rotation, the higher Tg: PS (phenyl) 100 °C < PVN (naphthyl) 135 °C < PVBP (biphenyl) 145 °C.

Q5Which one of the following polymers has the highest Tg (1) poly(methyl acrylate) (2) poly(methyl methacrylate) (3) poly(pentyl acrylate)

(2) PMMA (Tg ≈ 378 K).

  1. PMMA vs PMA: the extra α-CH₃ hinders backbone rotation → PMMA 378 K > PMA 279 K.
  2. Poly(pentyl acrylate): long flexible side chain → more free volume → lowest Tg.

Q6Which one of the following polymers has the highest Tg (1) polyethylene (2) polypropylene (3) polybutylene (4) polyhexylene (5) poly(4-methyl pentene)

(5) Poly(4-methyl pentene), Tg ≈ 302 K.

Its branched, bulky side group hinders bond rotation. Linear alkyl side chains are flexible and add free volume, so Tg falls as they lengthen (PP 253 > PB 249 > polyhexene 223 K); PE (no side group) 188 K.

Q7Which one of the following polymers has the lowest Tg (1) PMMA (2) PEMA (3) PPMA (4) PBMA

(4) PBMA (Tg ≈ −2 °C).

Each extra CH₂ in the ester side chain lowers Tg: PMMA 105 > PEMA 65 > PPMA 38 > PBMA −2 °C. Longer flexible side chains loosen chain packing and increase free volume (internal plasticization).

Q25Which one of the following PU (with the same MW and diol content) should have the lowest Tg (1) HMI (2) H12MDI (3) MDI (4) TDI

(1) HMI has the lowest Tg (HMI < H₁₂MDI < MDI ≈ TDI).

HMI is aliphatic –(CH₂)₆– with no ring → most flexible chain. Cycloaliphatic (H₁₂MDI) and aromatic (MDI, TDI) rings stiffen the hard segment → higher Tg.

Q10Estimate the Tg of 30 wt% di-n-butylphthalate (DBP) (Tg = 178 K) compounded with 70 wt% PVC (Tg = 355 K)?

Tg ≈ 273 K (0 °C).

Fox equation: 1/Tg = w₁/Tg₁ + w₂/Tg₂ = 0.30/178 + 0.70/355 = 3.657×10⁻³ K⁻¹ → Tg ≈ 273 K. The plasticizer lowers Tg of PVC from 355 K.

Q11Which ones of the followings are miscible blends (multiple choices) (1) PC/PBT (2) PS/PPO (3) PVDF/PMA (4) PEO/PMMA

(2), (3), (4) are miscible.

PS/PPO, PVDF/PMA and PEO/PMMA form one phase → a single Tg. (1) PC/PBT is immiscible → phase separated, two Tgs. Mixing needs ΔGm = ΔHm − TΔSm < 0; ΔSm of polymers is tiny, so specific interactions (ΔHm < 0) are required.

3. Structure and Tm

Q16The PTFE has lower melt energy than the PE does. (2860 J/mole of PTFE vs. 3870 J/mole of PE) How come the Tm of PTFE is much higher than PE? (645 K vs. 410 K) (Hint: Table 11.1 can help you)

Because PTFE has a much smaller melting entropy ΔSf.

Tm = ΔHf/ΔSf → ΔSf = ΔHf/Tm: PE 3870/410 = 9.44; PTFE 2860/645 = 4.43 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (about half).
PTFE is a stiff helical chain (bulky F atoms, large trans–gauche energy gap) → few conformations gained on melting → small ΔSf → high Tm despite lower ΔHf.

Q12Why is the Tm of isotactic PP higher than that of syndiotactic PP (187 vs. 150)?

Tm = ΔHf/ΔSf. Both iPP and sPP are regular and crystallize, but:

  1. iPP packs its 3₁ helices more tightly → larger ΔHf.
  2. sPP forms thinner, less perfect lamellae → Thompson–Gibbs Tm = Tm°[1 − 2σe/(l·Δhf)]: smaller l → lower Tm.

So iPP 187 °C > sPP 150 °C.

Q22Will the "regularity of configuration" affect more on Tg or Tm? Please use PP as an example to illustrate your point.

Regularity (tacticity) affects Tm much more than Tg.

  1. Tm: iPP and sPP are regular → crystallize → have Tm; atactic PP cannot crystallize → no Tm.
  2. Tg: depends on chain stiffness / pendant-group size; all three PP have the same –CH₃ → Tg ≈ −10 °C for all.

Q13Place the following polyurethane structures in order of decreasing Tm? (1) –HN–(CH2)6–NH–CO–O–(CH2)3–C6H4–(CH2)3–O–CO– (2) –HN–(CH2)8–NH–CO–O–(CH2)2–C6H4–(CH2)2–O–CO– (3) –HN–(CH2)8–NH–CO–O–(CH2)10–O–CO–

(1) > (2) > (3) (≈ 513 > 481–485 > 406 K), by Tm = ΔHf/ΔSf.

  1. ① (1), (2): phenylene ring stiffens the chain → small ΔSf → high Tm.
  2. ② (3): no ring, 18 flexible CH₂ → large ΔSf; longest repeat unit → fewest urethane H-bonds → small ΔHf → lowest.
  3. ③ (1) vs (2): same ring and CH₂ count; the shorter (CH₂)₆ diamine in (1) concentrates H-bonding → larger ΔHf → (1) > (2).

Q14Place the following Tm of the polyolefins with different side chain structures in a decreasing order and give a brief explanation (1) –CH3 (2) –CH2–C(CH3)2–CH2CH3 (3) –CH2CH2CH3 (4) –CH2CH2CH2CH3 (5) –CH2–CH(CH3)–CH2CH3

(2) > (5) > (1) > (3) > (4), by Tm = ΔHf/ΔSf.

  1. Bulky branched side groups near the backbone hinder rotation → stiff chain → small ΔSf → high Tm; (2) (quaternary C) is more crowded than (5).
  2. Linear flexible side chains: longer → larger ΔSf → lower Tm (CH₃ > propyl > butyl).

Q15Please list the Tm of the followings with the same MW in a decreasing order: (a) –[Ph–CO–Ph–O]– (b) –[Ph–CO–Ph–CO–Ph–O]– (c) –[Ph–O–Ph–CO–Ph–O]– (d) –[Ph–Ph–O–Ph–CO–Ph–O]–

(d) > (b) > (a) > (c) (689 > 657 > 640 > 628 K).

Stiffer backbone → smaller ΔSf → higher Tm = ΔHf/ΔSf. (d) has a directly linked biphenyl (stiffest); (b) has one more rigid C=O than (a); (c) has two flexible ether –O– links → lowest.

Q17Place the following Tm of nylon in a decreasing order and give brief explanation, Nylon 8, Nylon 9, Nylon 10, and Nylon 11.

Nylon 9 > 8 > 11 > 10 (482 > 473 > 463 > 461 K).

Larger n → lower amide density → fewer interchain H-bonds → lower ΔHf → lower Tm. Odd–even effect: odd-n nylon 9 aligns its H-bonds better, so 9 > 8; 11 and 10 differ by only ~2 K.

Q18Place the following Tm of polyester in a decreasing order [(CH2)mCOO]; m = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6

m = 2 > 3 = 5 > 4 > 6 (395 > 335 = 335 > 329 > 325 K).

Larger m → lower ester density and more flexible CH₂ → ΔHf↓, ΔSf↑ → Tm falls; m = 4 dips by the odd–even effect.

4. Crystallinity and barrier properties

Q3How to determine the crystallinity of a polymer?

  1. ① Density: φc = (ρ − ρa)/(ρc − ρa) (crystals are denser).
  2. ② X-ray diffraction (WAXD): area of sharp crystalline peaks vs the amorphous halo.
  3. ③ DSC: Xc = ΔHm/ΔHm° (melting-endotherm area ÷ 100 %-crystal value).

Q20How to determine the crystallinity of a polymer? How to determine the lamellae thickness of a polymeric crystal?

Crystallinity: density φc = (ρ − ρa)/(ρc − ρa); WAXD (crystalline peaks vs amorphous halo); DSC (ΔHm/ΔHm°).

Lamellar thickness l:

  1. ① DSC Tm + Thompson–Gibbs: Tm = Tm°[1 − 2σe/(l·Δhf)] → l = 2σeTm°/[Δhf(Tm° − Tm)]; thinner lamellae melt lower.
  2. ② SAXS: long period L = λ/(2 sin θ), l ≈ φc·L.
  3. ③ TEM / AFM imaging; Raman LAM. Typical l ≈ 10–20 nm.

Q38Which of the following polymers would expect to have the best barrier properties (hint: crystalline materials perform better than non-crystalline materials) (1) Atactic polystyrene (2) A random ethylene/propylene copolymer (50/50) composition. (3) mCOC (4) HDPE

(4) HDPE. Crystallization requires a regular chain.

  1. ① HDPE: linear –CH₂– chain, no side groups, few branches → packs easily → highest crystallinity (ρ = 0.94–0.97 g cm⁻³).
  2. ② Atactic PS: randomly placed phenyl groups → amorphous.
  3. ③ Random E/P copolymer: random sequence → amorphous.
  4. ④ mCOC: bulky random cyclic units → amorphous.
  5. ⑤ Crystallites are impermeable (lower S) and make a tortuous path (lower D) → P = D·S lowest for HDPE.

5. Stress–strain curves

Q1Please give a whole picture of the stress-strain curves for a semi-crystalline polymer with a polymer-morphology point of view.

  1. ① Linear, steep: amorphous tie chains stretch, lamellae bend slightly (elastic, σ = Eε).
  2. ② Yield point, then stress drops: lamellae tilt/slip, a neck forms.
  3. ③ Plateau: the neck propagates at nearly constant stress (cold drawing); lamellae break into blocks and align into microfibrils.
  4. ④ Strain hardening: chains are fully oriented, load carried by backbones.
  5. ⑤ Fracture (×).
  6. ⑥ (dashed) below Tg or fast pulling: brittle fracture, no yield.

Q2Please give a whole picture of the stress-strain curves for a rubbery polymer with a molecular conformation point of view.

Entropic elasticity: stretching reduces conformational entropy → retractive force, f = NkT(λ − λ⁻²).

  1. ① Low λ: obeys the Gaussian theory, low modulus — coiled chains are just uncoiled.
  2. ② λ ≳ 3: stress rises above theory (Mooney–Rivlin fits better).
  3. ③ Large λ: steep rise — chains are nearly straight but still crosslinked (strain-induced crystallization in NR).
  4. ④ Fracture at λ ≈ 7–8. Fully reversible up to break; no yield, no necking.
  5. ⑤ (dashed) Gaussian theory line.

6. Rubber elasticity

Q39In deriving the rubber elastic theory, what assumptions were used.

  1. ① Gaussian chains (long chains, end-to-end distance Gaussian).
  2. ② Force is purely entropic; internal-energy change ignored (ΔU = 0).
  3. ③ Affine deformation (crosslinks move with the macroscopic strain).
  4. ④ No volume change: λ₁λ₂λ₃ = 1.
  5. ⑤ Ideal network: no dangling ends or loops.
  6. ⑥ Isothermal and reversible.

Result: f = NkT(λ − λ⁻²).

Q33Pick up the right choices as follows (multiple choices) (1) Endothermic under tension (2) Modulus increases with temperature (3) Elasticity comes mainly from the entropy change (4) Adiabatic process is suitable to describe rubber under a large deformation?

(2), (3), (4).

  1. (1) Wrong: stretching lowers entropy → heat is released (exothermic).
  2. (2) Right: f ≈ −T(∂S/∂l) → modulus rises with T.
  3. (3) Right: elasticity comes from the entropy decrease.
  4. (4) Right: fast, large extension is reversible adiabatic (no time for heat exchange) → rubber warms up.

Q34Pick up the right choices as follows (multiple choices) for f = (∂U/∂l)T − T(∂S/∂l)T (1) (∂U/∂l)T is valid for crystalline and glassy state (2) (∂U/∂l)T represents the Hook's elasticity (3) T(∂S/∂l)T represents the Rubber elasticity (4) (∂U/∂l)T increases with temperature (5) For a given elongation, the (∂U/∂l)T is 104 higher than the T(∂S/∂l)T.

(1), (2), (3).

  1. (1)(2) Right: crystals and glasses deform by bond stretching, f ≈ (∂U/∂l)T = Hookean (energy) elasticity.
  2. (3) Right: −T(∂S/∂l)T is rubber (entropy) elasticity.
  3. (4) Wrong: the energy term is nearly independent of T; only the entropy term grows with T.
  4. (5) Wrong: in rubber the energy term is the smaller one (fU/f ≈ 0.1–0.2).

Q42Which one of the following statement regarding polyisoprene (natural rubber) is right (1) Natural rubber is mainly a trans form (2) The trans form has lower Tg (3) the repeat distance of cis is 0.88 nm (4) The Golfball covers can be made by Polyisoprene in trans form

Only (4).

  1. (1) Wrong: natural rubber is cis-1,4-polyisoprene.
  2. (2) Wrong: trans has the higher Tg (220 vs 200 K) and Tm.
  3. (3) Wrong: cis repeat distance is 0.81 nm; 0.88 nm is trans.
  4. (4) Right: hard, crystallizable trans-polyisoprene (gutta-percha) is used for golf-ball covers.

Q35Please give your brief reasons why the space shuttle Challenge exploded in 1988.

The launch (1986) was on a very cold morning. At low temperature the fluoroelastomer O-ring became stiff and lost its resiliency: it recovered its shape too slowly (Feynman’s ice-water demonstration). When the joint opened, the O-ring could not reseal → hot gas leaked and eroded the seal → the flame ignited the hydrogen fuel in the external tank.

Q36What is the "manufacturing secret" to make the "thermoplastic elastomer"?

A triblock copolymer: hard–soft–hard (e.g. SBS: PS–PB–PS).

  1. ① Hard end blocks (glassy or crystalline) phase-separate into domains = physical crosslinks.
  2. ② The low-Tg soft middle block links the domains → rubber elasticity at use temperature.
  3. ③ Above the hard-block Tg (or Tm) the domains soften → it flows and can be processed like a thermoplastic; the network re-forms on cooling (recyclable).

Q24Why does a SBS triblock copolymer exhibit a non-Newtonian and even highly elastic behavior at low temperature (< 423 K) but show a Newtonian behavior at 448 K?

Key: PS domains act as physical crosslinks at low T and disappear at high T.

  1. ① 448 K (above the order–disorder transition): PS and PB mix, no network → Newtonian plateau η₀ at low γ̇.
  2. ② 448 K: thins only at high γ̇, like a normal melt.
  3. ③ 423 K: PS blocks stay segregated in domains → physical network → yield-stress-like behaviour, no plateau (η ≈ τy/γ̇, slope ≈ −1): non-Newtonian and elastic.
  4. ④ 423 K, high γ̇: the network is broken → η drops toward the 448 K curve.

7. Viscoelasticity

Q27List four elastic characters of a viscoelastic polymer.

  1. ① Rod climbing (Weissenberg effect): the melt climbs a rotating rod (normal stresses); a Newtonian fluid dips.
  2. ② Die swell: the extrudate is thicker than the die.
  3. ③ Tubeless siphon: flow continues after the tube is lifted out of the liquid.
  4. ④ Secondary flow / vortices in a contraction (also: elastic recoil).

Cause: stretched chains store elastic energy (fading memory); Newtonian fluids show none of these.

Q31Pick up the right choices for describing the Relaxation Modulus vs. Temp. (Master curve) as follows (multiple choices) (1) Glassy modulus is 102 to 103 times higher than the Rubbery Modulus (2) Rubbery Modulus increases with the crosslinking degree (3) Rubbery Modulus increases with the crystallinity (4) the modulus of semicrystalline polymer remains independent of temperature if the crystallinity remains unchanged. (5) The crystallinity has little effect on the Glassy modulus

All five, (1)–(5), are correct.

  1. (1) Glassy ~10⁹ Pa (①) vs rubbery ~10⁶ Pa (③): 10²–10³ times.
  2. (2) Crosslinks keep the plateau (⑥, no flow); more crosslinks → higher plateau (G = ρRT/Mc).
  3. (3) Crystallites act as filler and crosslinks → higher modulus between Tg and Tm (⑦).
  4. (4) Constant crystallinity → plateau ⑦ nearly flat in T until Tm.
  5. (5) Below Tg the whole sample is already glassy → crystallinity has little effect.

① glassy ② transition ③ rubbery plateau ④ rubbery flow ⑤ viscous flow ⑥ crosslinked ⑦ semicrystalline

Q32How many Maxwell models in parallel at least can you apply to describe a typical Master curve (Relaxation Modulus vs. Temperature)? How to match the curve?

At least two Maxwell elements in parallel (τ₂ ≫ τ₁). One element has one τ and can drop only once; the master curve drops twice (glass → rubber → flow).

E(t) = E₁ e−t/τ₁ + E₂ e−t/τ₂, τᵢ = ηᵢ/Eᵢ

  1. ① t ≪ τ₁: Eg = E₁ + E₂.
  2. ② t ≈ τ₁: fast element relaxes, drop of E₁.
  3. ③ τ₁ ≪ t ≪ τ₂: plateau Er = E₂.
  4. ④ t ≈ τ₂: falls to zero (terminal flow).
  5. ⑤⑥ (dashed) the two single elements.

Matching: step heights give E₁, E₂; drop positions give τ₁, τ₂.

Q40The viscosity of polystyrene is 1012 Pa·s at 373 K. Determine the viscosity when the temperature increases to 473 K (WLF eq., a1 = 17.44, a2 = 51.6)

η(473 K) ≈ 3.1 Pa·s.

WLF with Tg = 373 K as reference, ΔT = 100 K:
log aT = −17.44 × 100 / (51.6 + 100) = −11.5 → aT = 3.1×10⁻¹²
η = aT × η(Tg) = 3.1×10⁻¹² × 10¹² ≈ 3.1 Pa·s

8. Melt rheology

Q26What is shear thinning? What is shear thickening? Why a polymer behaves a shear thinning rather than shear thickening.

Power law: η = m·γ̇n−1 (n = 1: Newtonian).

  1. Shear thinning: n < 1, η decreases as γ̇ increases (solid line ③).
  2. Shear thickening: n > 1, η increases as γ̇ increases (dashed ④, e.g. concentrated suspensions).
  3. Why polymers thin: at high γ̇ the chains cannot relax, so they uncoil, align in the flow direction and disentangle → less flow resistance. There is no mechanism (like particle jamming) that would make them thicken.

Q23Why does a polymer melt usually exhibit a shear thinning behavior?

  1. ① Low γ̇ (γ̇ < 1/λ): chains relax in time → Newtonian plateau η₀.
  2. ② γ̇ ≈ 1/λ: the curve bends down.
  3. ③ High γ̇: chains cannot relax → uncoil, align and disentangle → η = m·γ̇n−1, n < 1 (shear thinning).

Q28What is the relationship between the molecular weight and the melt viscosity?

  1. ① M < Mc: no entanglement, η₀ ∝ M (slope 1).
  2. ② Break at Mc ≈ 2–3 Me.
  3. ③ M > Mc: entanglement, reptation → η₀ ∝ M3.4 (slope 3.4). Doubling M → η₀ × 23.4 ≈ 10.6.

Q29What will the melt viscosity be affected by (1) temperature (2) pressure (3) shear rate (4) molecular weight (5) plasticizer wt%

η decreases with (1) T↑, (3) γ̇↑, (5) plasticizer↑; η increases with (2) pressure↑, (4) M↑.

  1. (1) Higher T → more free volume → η↓ (WLF / Doolittle η = A exp(B/Vf)).
  2. (2) Higher pressure → less free volume → η↑.
  3. (3) Higher γ̇ → chains orient and disentangle → shear thinning.
  4. (4) Higher M → more friction and entanglement: η₀ ∝ M (< Mc), ∝ M3.4 (> Mc).
  5. (5) Plasticizer → more free volume, lower Tg → η↓.

Q30Please explain the Cox-Merz rule.

Cox–Merz rule: the steady-shear viscosity equals the magnitude of the complex viscosity at the same numerical value of shear rate and angular frequency:

η(γ̇) ≈ |η*(ω)| at γ̇ = ω,   |η*| = √(G′² + G″²)/ω

So a small-amplitude frequency sweep (G′, G″) can predict the steady flow curve (two different methods give nearly the same viscosity). It is empirical: works for homogeneous linear melts; may fail for filled systems, gels and block copolymers.

9. Fracture mechanics

Q41Considering an aluminum plate has Kc of 44 MPa m1/2, thickness 20 mm and width 100 mm, with 40 mm crack. What is the critical limit for the plate of load (kN)? (the geometry factor Y = 2.1)

Fc ≈ 118 kN.

Fracture when K = Kc, with K = Yσ√(πa), σ = F/(WB):
Fc = Kc·W·B / (Y√(πa)) = (44×10⁶ × 0.100 × 0.020) / (2.1 × √(π × 0.040))
= 88 000 / (2.1 × 0.3545) ≈ 1.18×10⁵ N ≈ 118 kN
